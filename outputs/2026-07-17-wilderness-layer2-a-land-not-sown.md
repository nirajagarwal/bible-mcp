# A Land Not Sown

**1. Two ways to be empty.** Hebrew keeps a word for wilderness as a place and a word for wilderness as a sentence. *Midbar* occurs 270 times, and its meaning barely moves — "wilderness, desert," verse after verse; what varies is only what happens there. Numbers owns it (48 occurrences), then Exodus, then the prophets who re-read the wandering. *Shemamah* is the other word, and it is not a place at all but a verdict: "a desolation," what a land *becomes* under judgment — Leviticus's threat, Jeremiah's grief, Ezekiel's refrain. The corpus turns on this distinction. Being in the wilderness and being made a wilderness are different fates, and nearly everything tender in the tradition happens in the first.

**2. The land just past the last furrow.** The *midbar* is not the Sahara. It is steppe — the corpus speaks of "the pastures of the wilderness" (Joel 1:19-20), grazing land good for flocks and useless for plows, beginning at the line where rainfall becomes too scant and too erratic for sowing. Jeremiah defines it exactly that way, by negation of agriculture: "how you followed Me in the wilderness, in a land not sown" (Jer 2:2). That is why the wilderness narratives are provision stories — manna, quail, water out of the flinty rock (Exod 16; Wis 11:4). The land defined by the absence of human provision becomes the theater where provision must come from somewhere else.

**3. Three words deep, and tender.** Deuteronomy 32:10 piles up the vocabulary — *ʾereṣ midbar*, then *tohu*, then *yeshimon*: "in a land of wilderness, and in a wasteland, a howling, of a desolate place." *Tohu* is Genesis 1:2's "formless"; the verse reaches for cosmology to say how empty this place was. And precisely there, at the stack's bottom: "he surrounded it, he took care of it, he guarded it like the pupil of his eye." Three desolation words where the tenderness is maximal — the corpus's own juxtaposition. The Hebrew for "pupil" is *ʾishon*, by the standard lexicon reading a diminutive, "little man of the eye" — named for the tiny reflection of yourself you see in someone else's pupil. That is a lexicon tradition, not a corpus datum; but if it holds, the verse says the wilderness is where you are close enough to the watcher to appear in his eye.

**4. The braid.** Tradition has long heard *dabar*, "speak," inside *midbar*. That link is folk etymology, not philology — the roots' histories do not support it, and it should be said plainly. But the corpus data underneath the folk instinct is real and checkable. *Dabar* occurs 1,136 times, and its largest single-book count is Numbers — 119, ahead of Jeremiah and Exodus — a raw count, not a normalized density, but still: the speaking-word is thickest in the wilderness book. Deuteronomy 8:3 makes the collocation doctrine: in the wilderness man lives "by every utterance of the mouth of Yahweh." And Hosea sets the verb inside the noun in a single clause — MT 2:16 (English 2:14): *veholachtiha hammidbar vedibbarti al-libbah*, "I will lead her to the wilderness and I will *speak* to her heart" — with Jeremiah 2:2's bridal wilderness as its answering echo. Whatever the etymology, the corpus keeps putting the divine voice in the empty place.

**5. Where "in the wilderness" attaches.** Isaiah 40:3 in the Masoretic text reads: *qol qoreʾ* — "a voice is calling" — then "in the wilderness clear the way of Yahweh; in the *arabah* a highway for our God." The parallelism binds "in the wilderness" to the road-building, not the voice: it is the way, not the herald, that is out there. All three Gospel quotations attach it the other way: "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (Matt 3:3; Mark 1:3; John 1:23). That the Gospels follow the Septuagint's construal is standard scholarship, but the corpus contains no Septuagint, so the middle term stays unverified; only the two poles are checked. Outside the corpus, training knowledge adds a third reader: the Qumran community's Rule (1QS 8) quotes the verse with the Masoretic construal and obeys it literally — go to the wilderness, prepare the way — and glosses the way-preparing as the study of Torah. One question of where a phrase attaches, three vocations: a road crew, a herald, a desert full of scribes. Every reading agrees on the venue.

**6. The Greek splits noun from verb.** New Testament *erēmos* (47 occurrences) hosts the whole hospitable range: theophany, temptation, feeding miracles, refuge, prayer — and in Galatians 4:27, quoting Isaiah 54:1, "the desolate woman" who bears more children than the married one, which ties the Greek word straight into Isaiah 62:4's marriage arc, where a land called Desolate is renamed Beulah, "married." But the verb *erēmoō* and its action-noun *erēmōsis* are uniformly catastrophic: kingdoms "brought to desolation" (Matt 12:25), Babylon stripped and burned (Rev 17:16), the "abomination of desolation" (Matt 24:15). The place-word blesses; the process-word only destroys. Modern environmental science ended up drawing the same line: deserts are ecosystems, desertification is a degradation — the noun names a habitat, the verb names a verdict. The Greek testament made the Hebrew's *midbar*/*shemamah* distinction grammatical.

**7. Proof load.** Why the wilderness at all? Deuteronomy 8:2 answers with three verbs — *ʿanah*, *nasah*, *yadaʿ*: forty years "so that He might humble you and test you in order to know what was in your heart." Engineering has an exact name for this: proof testing. You load a vessel beyond its working pressure not to break it but because latent flaws are invisible at rest; only stress makes the interior state knowable. The corpus's own cross-references run the same logic forward — the testing of faith producing endurance (Jas 1:3), faith "of greater worth than gold" proved genuine by fire (1 Pet 1:7). The wilderness is the proof load: not punishment, instrumentation.

**8. Refuge.** The empty land shelters what the sown land expels. Hagar, a runaway slave, is found by a spring in the wilderness (Gen 16:7) — and she, of all people in Genesis, is the one who gives God a name: El Roi, "the God who sees me" (Gen 16:13). Her son becomes the wilderness archer (Gen 21:20). David hides behind Jeshimon from Saul. By the Maccabean crisis the register militarizes: "many who sought justice and judgment went down into the wilderness to live there" (1 Macc 2:29) — the survey infers, from training knowledge rather than the corpus, that this move anticipates Qumran. The pattern outlived the wars and kept its economics: in Roman Egypt, *anachōrēsis* — "withdrawal," the word that eventually gave monasticism its "anchorite" — first shows up in the papyri as what peasants did when taxes broke them: they fled to the desert. Athanasius could later boast that the monks had made the desert a city; the city's founders included tax refugees. And Revelation keeps the register to the end: the woman flees "into the wilderness, where God had prepared a place for her" (Rev 12:6) — whether "prepared" there shares its verb with "make ready the way" in Mark 1:3 is a single-word link the survey flagged but has not yet checked.

**9. The machine runs both directions.** Psalm 107 operates the reversal within a single poem: "He turns rivers into deserts" (v. 33), "He turns a desert into a pool of water" (v. 35). Isaiah promises the second motion three times over — the desert blossoming (35:1), rivers on barren heights (41:18), "a way in the wilderness" (43:19). Desert ecology gives the promise an uncanny literal analogue: arid soils hold seed banks, dormant for years, that a single rain detonates into bloom — the Atacama's *desierto florido*, Death Valley's superblooms. The desert that flowers was never quite empty; it was waiting. The corpus's version is a promise, not a botany lecture — but both insist that desolation can be a stored state rather than a final one.

**10. The inversion.** The later tradition swallows the theme whole. Bunyan opens his allegory inside it — "As I walked through the wilderness of this world" — wilderness as the frame of everything, not an episode. Augustine wanted the literal desert and reports, "I had purposed to flee to the wilderness: but Thou forbadest me" (Confessions 10.70); denied the geography, he finds it internalized, a "so vast wilderness, full of snares" (10.56), knowing the real deserts only secondhand through Antony's story (8.16). 1 Clement, for all its Exodus examples, has no wilderness material at all — a genuine, verified absence. And then Julian of Norwich performs the tradition's most startling reversal: in her lord-and-servant shewing it is the *Lord* who sits "on the earth, barren and desert, alone in wilderness," waiting in fallen man's soul — "without the Lord was nothing but wilderness" — until "now sitteth not the Son on earth in wilderness" (Julian 60). Gestalt psychology calls this a figure-ground reversal — Rubin's famous image, 1915, vase or two faces depending on which region you read as figure. Every earlier text put humanity in the wilderness waiting for God to speak. Julian flips figure and ground without changing the picture: God in the wilderness, and the wilderness is us.

**Coda.** So the survey's question comes back with its edge intact. At every layer the corpus pairs the wilderness with the voice: the speaking-word thickest in the wilderness book, "every utterance of the mouth of Yahweh" as wilderness food, "I will speak to her heart" inside the *midbar*, the herald — or the road — of Isaiah 40:3, John's word coming "in the wilderness" (Luke 3:2). Meanwhile the verb family, the making-desolate words, only ever means being emptied. Is emptiness, then, the corpus's precondition for address — must a place, or a people, first be un-inhabited before it can be spoken to? Chesterton supplies the caution against romancing the void: "a mere unmeaning wilderness is not even impressive." Empty is not yet eloquent. And Mark supplies the fact that will not resolve into either answer: very early, while it was still dark, the one whom the Gospels call the Word "went out to a solitary place" — *erēmos* — "and there he prayed" (Mark 1:35). Even the Voice, when it wanted to speak and be spoken to, walked out past the last furrow. Whether the emptiness made the address possible, or the address is what kept the emptiness from being mere — the corpus never says. It only keeps returning to the same coordinates: a land not sown; a voice, calling.
