**The core cluster and the sharpest single fact.** Hebrew has one dominant verb for the "hidden" side of this theme: סָתַר (*sathar*, H5641), 81 occurrences, glossed across "hide," "hid," "hiding himself," "he will hide." Its distribution is telling — Psalms 23x, Isaiah 14x, Job 8x, Deuteronomy 6x, 1 Samuel 6x, Proverbs 5x — a spread across lament, prophecy, wisdom, and narrative, not concentrated in one genre. The single most load-bearing verse in the whole corpus for this theme is **Deuteronomy 29:29**, and the interlinear shows why it is built for the job: *ha-nnistārōt* ("the hidden things," a Niphal participle of *sathar*, passive — things *that have been hidden*, not things someone is actively hiding) stand in a two-beat parallel against *ha-nniḡlōṯ* ("the revealed things," a Niphal participle of גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540). Both participles are passive and both are Niphal — the text is not staging God-hides versus God-reveals as competing verbs so much as naming two settled *states*: the concealed and the disclosed, each assigned an owner. "The secret things belong to Yahweh our God; but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law." This is jurisdictional language, almost legal — a boundary marker, not a mystery to be solved. Ownership, not epistemology, is the verse's real subject.

**Galah's double life is the richest single philological find in this survey.** H1540 occurs 186-187 times, and its gloss range is dominated not by "reveal" but by "uncover" and "exile": "you will uncover" (15x), "he took into exile" (11x), "I will uncover" (9x), "uncover" (7x). Distribution: Jeremiah 26x, Leviticus 24x, Isaiah 17x, 2 Kings 15x, Amos 12x. The Leviticus weight is the "uncovering nakedness" laws (Lev 18, 20 — forbidden sexual exposures use this verb constantly); the Jeremiah/Kings weight is the verb's other major sense, "carry into exile." So the same root that gives us "the LORD reveals His plan to His servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7) is, by frequency, first and foremost a verb of shameful exposure and national deportation. Revelation, in Hebrew's own vocabulary, is kin to nakedness laid bare and to a people stripped from their land — disclosure is not neutral information-transfer, it is a species of exposure. This lines up, unprompted, with the Greek: ἀποκαλύπτω (*apokalyptō*, "to reveal," G601, 26x — Luke 5x, Matthew 4x, Romans 3x) is literally *apo* + *kalyptō*, "away" + "to cover" — **to un-cover**. Two unrelated languages independently built their "reveal" vocabulary out of "uncover." This is a philological fact, not an inference — both roots are attested doing exactly this double duty in the corpus's own data — and it means the corpus's hidden/revealed axis is really a covered/uncovered axis wearing theological clothes.

**Isaiah 45 holds the tension inside a single chapter, which is itself worth flagging as a control.** Verse 15: "Truly You are a God who hides Himself (*mistattēr*, a Hitpael participle of *sathar* — reflexive, "hides *Himself*," an action God performs on Himself, not something done to Him), O God of Israel, the Savior." Four verses later, same speaker, same chapter: "I have not spoken in secret, from a place in a land of darkness. I did not say to the descendants of Jacob, 'Seek Me in a wasteland.' I, the LORD, speak the truth; I say what is right" (Isa 45:19). The chapter does not resolve this into a contradiction to be explained away — it holds both claims as true of the same God in the same breath: self-concealing, and also not-secretive. The Hitpael grammar in v.15 matters here — it is not that Israel fails to find God, but that hiding is something God does *to Himself*, an act of will, which is exactly the frame the surrounding "Savior" epithet and v.19's "I have not spoken in secret" require: the hiding is not evasion, it sits beside plain speech in the same mouth. compare_versions on 45:15 shows BSB and WEB rendering *mistattēr* almost identically ("a God who hides Himself" / "a God who has hidden yourself") — no translation-tradition fork here, the ambiguity is in the text itself, not introduced by any version.

**Aramaic supplies a third root, and Daniel 2 is where all three converge.** רָז (*raz*, H7328, "mystery/secret," 9x) is confined entirely to Daniel (chs. 2 and 4) — this is a loanword (ultimately Persian) that Hebrew narrative reaches for only in the Babylonian court setting, nowhere else in the Tanakh. Daniel 2:22's interlinear is dense with the theme's whole vocabulary in one verse: *gāleʾ* ("revealing," from גלה/*galah*, the same root as Deut 29:29's "revealed things") stands next to *məsattərāṯ* — and here is a genuine cross-linguistic control worth running: the Aramaic word glossed "hidden" in this verse is tagged **H5642 (סְתַר)**, not H5641. It is the Aramaic cognate of the Hebrew *sathar* root, appearing under its own Strong's number because Daniel 2 is written in Aramaic, not Hebrew — same consonantal root, different language-tag, correctly split by the tool. So Daniel 2:22 reads: "He reveals [galah] the deep and hidden [setar, cognate of sathar] things; He knows what lies in darkness, and light dwells with Him" — the verse fuses reveal-root, hide-root, and the light/darkness pair in eleven Aramaic words, then the surrounding narrative supplies the third leg, *raz*, "mystery": Daniel is repeatedly called "a revealer of secrets" (2:47) and God "the Revealer of Mysteries" (2:29) — a title, not just a verb. Job 12:22 supplies a near-verbatim Hebrew echo of the same line — "He reveals the deep things of darkness and brings deep shadows into light" — using *galah* again, in a completely different book and genre (Job's disputation vs. Daniel's court-tale), suggesting this exact reveals-what-is-dark-into-light collocation was stock theological idiom, not a one-off.

**Proverbs 25:2 is the wisdom-literature counterpart to Deuteronomy 29:29, and it too uses *sathar*.** "It is the glory of God to conceal [*hastēr*, Hiphil imperative-infinitive of *sathar*] a matter, and the glory of kings to search it out [*ḥăqōr*, from חָקַר, 'to search/investigate']." Same root as "the God who hides Himself" and as Deuteronomy's "hidden things," but now recast as a *virtue* — concealment is glorious, not merely a fact about God's nature. get_cross_references on this verse pulls almost the same cluster as Deut 29:29 (Rom 11:33-34, Job 11:6-7, Job 42:3 — "who conceals My counsel without knowledge? ... things too wonderful for me to know"), confirming these two verses function as paired hubs in the corpus's own citation network, not just in my reading of them. **A direct rewrite of Proverbs 25:2 turns up in the Apocrypha**, and it inverts the proverb's logic rather than repeating it: "It is good to conceal the secret of a king, but to reveal gloriously the works of God" (Tob 12:7, spoken by the angel Raphael as he unveils his own identity). Proverbs praises concealment as royal glory across the board; Tobit splits the maxim in two — conceal *human* secrets, but *reveal* God's works gloriously. The shared vocabulary ("glory... conceal... reveal") makes the divergence pointed rather than coincidental.

**Wisdom itself is repeatedly the hidden object, in a register distinct from the "secret things belong to God" jurisdictional language.** Job 28 — the standalone poem on where wisdom is found — states flatly: "It is hidden from the eyes of every living thing and concealed from the birds of the air" (28:21), and resolves not into a search technique but into "God understands its way, and He knows its place" (28:23), landing at 28:28: fear of the Lord is wisdom, full stop — access to the deposit itself is closed off, but a substitute (reverence) is offered in its place. Sirach doubles this twice in near-identical language: "Wisdom that is hidden, and treasure that is out of sight — what profit is in either of them?" (Sir 20:30, repeated at 41:14) — practical wisdom literature's answer to Job 28 is almost impatient: hidden wisdom is *useless* wisdom, so don't prize concealment for its own sake. Paul picks up the same hidden-wisdom-as-treasure image and inverts its value: "we speak God's wisdom in a mystery, the wisdom that has been hidden, which God foreordained before the worlds for our glory" (1 Cor 2:7) — quoting Isaiah 64:4 in v.9 ("no eye has seen, no ear has heard...") as prooftext — and "in whom [Christ] all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden" (Col 2:3). Sirach says hidden wisdom profits no one; Paul says the hidden wisdom is *for our glory* and has now been disclosed by the Spirit (1 Cor 2:10). Same image, opposite verdict — the register shift is the New Testament claiming that the specific hiddenness Job and Sirach lament has, in this one case, ended.

**μυστήριον (*mystērion*, G3466, 27x — Eph 6x, 1 Cor 5x, Rev 4x, Col 4x) is Paul's technical term for exactly this now-ended hiddenness**, and its regular grammar is a hidden→revealed movement compressed into a single clause: "the mystery that was hidden for ages and generations but is now revealed to His saints" (Col 1:26); "the mystery concealed for ages past but now revealed and made known" (Rom 16:25-26); "which for ages past was kept hidden in God" (Eph 3:9). The verb doing the "revealed" half of this pairing is regularly φανερόω (*phaneroō*, G5319, "to manifest," 49x — 1 John 9x, 2 Cor 9x, John 9x), not ἀποκαλύπτω — worth noting as a register distinction: *apokalyptō* tends toward disclosure-of-content (an unveiling of what was covered), *phaneroō* toward appearing-in-visible-form (1 John 1:2's "the life was revealed... we have seen"; 1 Tim 3:16's "He appeared in the flesh"). Both verbs answer to *kryptō*/*kryptos*, but they answer different questions — one "what was it hiding," the other "has it become visible."

**Greek κρύπτω (*kryptō*, G2928, 19x) and its adjective κρυπτός (*kryptos*, G2927, 19x — two lemmas sharing one Strong's number, κρυπτός 17x plus κρυφαῖος 2x) carry the bulk of Gospel-material hiddenness, and three distinct registers sit inside this one root:**

- *Parable/kingdom register*: "The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field" (Matt 13:44); "I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world" (Matt 13:35, explicitly framed as prophecy-fulfillment); "the meaning was hidden from them" (Luke 18:34, of the disciples failing to grasp the passion prediction) — hiddenness here is pedagogical, tied to parable-form itself: "to you it is given to know the mysteries of the kingdom... but to others I speak in parables" (Luke 8:10 = Mark 4:11), meaning the *same teaching* is simultaneously revelation and concealment depending on the hearer. This is the sharpest form of the tension in the whole survey: one speech-act, two opposite effects, by design.
- *Ethical/reversal register, tied to light*: "There is nothing hidden that will not be disclosed... brought to light" appears four times near-verbatim — Mark 4:22, Luke 8:17, Luke 12:2-3, Matt 10:26 — find_similar on Luke 8:17 confirms Mark 4:22 (0.990) and Luke 12:2 (0.972) as its two closest matches in the whole corpus by embedding similarity, the tightest cluster surfaced in this survey. The saying's own logic is that hiddenness is *temporary by nature* — not that some things are hidden and some revealed, but that concealment is a stage every hidden thing passes through on its way to disclosure ("what you have spoken in the ear in inner rooms will be proclaimed on the housetops," Luke 12:3). Matthew 5:14-15 supplies the positive command version of the same logic: "You are the light of the world. A city on a hill cannot be hidden" — followed immediately by the lamp-not-under-a-basket saying — light *structurally* cannot stay hidden, and disciples are told they share that structural property.
- *Voluntary-secrecy-as-piety register*: Matthew 6:1-18's triple refrain (alms, prayer, fasting — vv.4, 6, 18) commands *human* concealment: give, pray, and fast "in secret" (κρυπτός each time), specifically so only "your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you." This is the one place in the corpus where *hiding* is the commanded human behavior and *God's seeing into secrecy* — not God's revealing — is the operative divine action. get_cross_references on Matt 6:4 pulls Ps 139:1-3 and 139:12 ("even the darkness doesn't hide from you... darkness is as light to you") and Jer 17:10 ("I, the LORD, search the heart") — the hub here is God's penetrating sight, not God's disclosure. This register inverts Job 28/Sirach's "hidden wisdom is useless": here hiddenness *from other people* is precisely what makes an act valuable before God.

**Jeremiah 23:24 and Psalm 139:11-12 form a tight pair on the impossibility of hiding from God**, both using *sathar*: "Can a man hide in secret places where I cannot see him? ... Do I not fill the heavens and the earth?" (Jer 23:24) and "If I say, 'Surely the darkness will hide me...' even the darkness is not dark to You, but the night shines like the day" (Ps 139:11-12). Both function as rebuttals to an imagined human strategy of hiding *from* God — a third sense of "hidden" distinct from mystery-hidden-then-revealed and from secret-piety: hiddenness attempted, and refused. Genesis 3:10 ("I heard Your voice in the garden, and I was afraid... so I hid myself") is the narrative root of this register — the first hiding in the corpus's own story-order is a human fleeing God's presence, not a divine act of concealment. Amos 9:3, seen in the initial word_study batch for *sathar*, escalates this into a hunt: "though they hide themselves atop Carmel, there I will track them... though they hide from Me at the bottom of the sea, there I will command the serpent to bite them" — the same theological point as Jer 23:24 and Ps 139, but in judgment-oracle register rather than psalm or prophecy-of-comfort register.

**The Messianic-secret cluster (Mark 1:44, 5:43, 7:36; Matt 17:9) is a distinct phenomenon that doesn't use *kryptō* vocabulary at all** — these are imperatives ("tell no one," "he ordered them not to tell anyone"), not statements about something being hidden. It belongs to the theme thematically (Jesus actively withholding disclosure of his identity/works) but lexically it's a different construction — worth flagging because it would be easy to conflate with the *kryptō* material and the corpus data shows they're not the same mechanism. Mark 7:36 supplies a wry footnote to the whole theme: "the more He ordered them, the more widely they proclaimed it" — commanded concealment failing on contact with the same structural principle Matt 5:14 states positively (light cannot stay hidden). Luke 19:20's mina "laid away in a piece of cloth" (a *sudarion*, face-cloth, not "the ground" as in Matthew's talent parable, and no κρύπτω-family verb used at all in the Luke telling) is a genuine textual variant worth flagging: the two parables that both get folk-remembered as "the servant who hid the money" use different concealment vocabulary and different objects entirely — Matthew's servant digs in earth and uses *kryptō* explicitly (25:18, 25); Luke's servant merely "laid away" (ἀποτίθημι-family, not checked here) in cloth. The traditional conflation of the two parables under one "hidden talent" heading is a harmonizing habit of preaching, not a fact the text supports as tightly as assumed.

**Colossians 3:3 ("your life is hidden with Christ in God") sits in a different register from every other κρύπτω occurrence** — not a mystery awaiting disclosure, not a virtue of secrecy, not an evasion — a settled state of belonging, grammatically a perfect passive ("has been hidden and remains so"), paired two verses earlier with "you died" and immediately followed by "When Christ, who is your life, appears [φανερόω again], then you also will appear with him in glory" (3:4, not separately fetched here but visible in the surrounding cross-reference data) — meaning even this most static-sounding "hidden" verse is still, true to the theme's pattern everywhere else in this survey, explicitly pointed toward a future *phaneroō*-event. I did not find a single occurrence across this survey of "hidden" used without an implied or explicit future disclosure attached to it, in either testament — that absence (of permanently, irrevocably hidden things, outside of Eccl 12:14's judgment-context and the human-hiding-from-God register) may be the most structurally significant finding, and is stated as a claim to check below, not asserted as proven.

**The patristic layer engages this theme directly, and not merely by quoting it — it structures Irenaeus's whole polemical method.** Irenaeus opens *Against Heresies* Book I by explicitly citing "there is nothing hidden which shall not be revealed, nor secret that shall not be made known" (his own paraphrase, closely tracking Luke 8:17/Matt 10:26) as his personal warrant for publishing the Valentinians' "hidden mysteries, which they keep in silence among themselves" (IREN-AH1.31.4) — Irenaeus recruits the corpus's own hidden→revealed logic as a weapon: heretical secrecy will inevitably be exposed, so exposing it himself is participating in a structural inevitability the text itself promises, not merely a personal choice. This is a genuinely new register not visible anywhere in the biblical material itself: *hiddenness as a marker of illegitimacy* — the Gnostics' secrecy is itself evidence against them, whereas every biblical instance of divine or Christic hiddenness (Isa 45:15, the parable-hiddenness of Luke 8:10, Col 3:3) is treated as proper to God's own nature or economy, never as suspect. get_citations on the two obvious NT hub-verses (Luke 8:17, Col 1:26) returned *no* results from the citation index, consistent with the known sparse coverage of that tool (documented as covering only two patristic works via footnote extraction) — but full-text `search` on IREN-AH1 found the Luke 8:17/Matt 10:26 quotation directly, meaning this is one more case (per the skill's own prior-runs note) of the citation index under-recalling a real quotation that full-text search catches. The Imitation of Christ, four centuries downstream, takes the opposite institutional stance from Irenaeus: rather than "expose what is hidden," its refrain is "meditation upon the hidden judgments of God, that we may not be lifted up" (title of ch. 51) and "why this man is thus left, and that man is taken into so great favour... these things pass all man's power of judging" (95.2) — hiddenness here is not a mystery destined for disclosure or a mark of heresy, but a permanent limit on human judgment that piety must simply accept, closer in spirit to Job 28's "hidden from the eyes of every living thing" than to Daniel 2's Revealer-of-Mysteries or Colossians' now-revealed mystery. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* uses "mystery" and "revealed" constantly but almost entirely typologically (the cross prefigured in Joseph's blessing, the scarlet thread as the blood of Christ) — a fourth register again, hiddenness as *textual latency*, meaning already lodged in the OT text awaiting a Christological key, distinct from Daniel's hidden-future-events, Paul's hidden-in-God-before-creation, and the Gospels' hidden-in-parable-form.

**The synthesis question the corpus itself poses:** is "hidden" ever, anywhere in this corpus, a *permanent* category — or does every real instance of hiddenness (excepting only the human attempt to hide *from* God, which the text treats as futile rather than as a stable state) carry, explicitly or by the logic of its own context, a built-in trajectory toward disclosure?

**Checkable claims:**
1. That no biblical (BSB/WEB canonical) occurrence of κρύπτω/κρυπτός or סָתַר describing something *God* hides (as opposed to something a person hides from God, or human secrecy commanded as piety) lacks an explicit or contextually immediate disclosure-clause — this survey found none, but did not exhaustively check all 81 *sathar* occurrences or all 19 *kryptō* occurrences individually against this claim; a full pass would need to.
2. That Matthew's talent parable (25:14-30) and Luke's minas parable (19:12-27) differ specifically in that Matthew's hiding servant uses κρύπτω + "in the ground" while Luke's uses "laid away... in a piece of cloth" with no κρύπτω-family verb — confirmed here by direct get_passage comparison of Matt 25:18/25:25 vs Luke 19:20, worth a fuller Greek-word check on the Luke verb itself.
3. That H5642 (סְתַר) in Dan 2:22 is specifically the Aramaic cognate of H5641 (סָתַר), triggered by Daniel 2's Aramaic-language section — confirmed by the interlinear tagging in this survey; worth verifying against a grammar reference that Daniel's Aramaic section boundary (2:4b onward) is what accounts for the tag switch.
4. That ἀποκαλύπτω is etymologically "away/off" (ἀπό) + "to cover" (καλύπτω), i.e. literally "uncover" — standard Greek lexicography, not verified against a lexicon tool in this survey (bible-mcp has no dedicated etymology tool); worth an external check.

**Gaps noticed:**
- `word_study` failed on a bare numeric query for G601 (ἀποκαλύπτω) — both `"G601"` and zero-padded `"G0601"` returned "No occurrences found" — despite the word demonstrably existing in the corpus (confirmed via `get_interlinear` on Matt 11:25, which tags "ἀπεκάλυψας" with lemma=ἀποκαλύπτω G601) and despite querying by the lemma text itself (`"ἀποκαλύπτω"`) succeeding and returning all 26 occurrences correctly tagged as G601. This looks like a genuine numeric-Strong's-lookup bug specific to this lemma (unclear if isolated or systemic) rather than a padding issue — worth flagging to maintainers.
- `get_passage` rejected `Tob.12.7` with an unhelpful-until-retried error ("No text found... Apocrypha books are only in version='WEB'") when called without an explicit `version` parameter, even though the tool clearly knows which version the book requires — it could auto-route rather than error. Retrying with `version=WEB` succeeded immediately.
- `get_citations` returned zero results for both hub verses tested (Luke 8:17, Col 1:26), consistent with the documented sparse coverage (only two patristic works indexed via footnote extraction) — and, as previously observed in other survey runs, full-text `search` on the patristic works found a real quotation (Irenaeus quoting Luke 8:17/Matt 10:26 in IREN-AH1.0.2) that the citation index missed. This is at least the fourth such miss logged across this pipeline's survey runs and suggests the citation index gap is systemic rather than occasional.
- The Apocrypha/WEB layer engages "hidden wisdom" and "reveal the works of God" directly (Tobit, Sirach, 2 Esdras) but I did not sweep Wisdom of Solomon or 1–2 Maccabees specifically for this theme beyond what surfaced incidentally in semantic_search — a targeted `search` pass over Wisdom of Solomon in particular (its "hidden things" / "secrets of God" language in chs. 1–9) would likely add material.
- I did not run `entities_in_passage`/`get_entity` — this theme has no recurring place or person that anchors it the way Egypt anchors wilderness or the potter's house anchors Jer 18; Daniel, Isaiah, and Paul each appear but as authors/speakers across many unrelated passages, not as an entity-cluster worth isolating.
- CONFESSIONS (Augustine) and JULIAN (Julian of Norwich) — two other prose-classic layers likely to carry strong "hidden things of God" material given their contemplative genre — were not searched in this survey; a follow-up pass should check both before this theme moves to Layer 2.
