# What the Clay Is Allowed to Say

## 1. The forbidden sentence

"Shall what is formed say of him who formed it, 'He has no understanding'?" (Isaiah 29:16). Isaiah asks it as a taunt — you have turned things upside down, as though the clay could file a complaint against the potter. Sixteen chapters later, Isaiah 45:9-11 repeats the ban on the questioning creature, and Paul carries it into Greek: "will the thing formed say to him who formed it?" (Romans 9:20). Fourteen centuries after that, Thomas à Kempis is still enforcing it: "For how shall the clay boast against Him that fashioned it?" (Imitation 51.5).

And yet the hub of this whole theme — the verse the cross-reference voters link to Jeremiah 18, Isaiah 29:16 and 45:9, Romans 9, and Psalm 119:73 — is a verse in which the clay speaks. "We are the clay, and You are the potter" (Isaiah 64:8; the Hebrew sits at MT 64:7). Same vocabulary, same two parties, opposite register: not the creature interrogating upward but the creature conceding everything and asking anyway, a petition prayed from inside ruin. Job 10:9 is its private twin — "Remember that You molded me like clay. Would You now return me to dust?" — and the corpus never rebukes either speaker.

So the live tension in this material is not whether God is the potter; everyone in the corpus grants that. It is what the clay is allowed to say — rebuked when it questions, heard when it prays — and, behind that, the harder question the survey leaves exposed at the end: whether the clay is ever allowed to *do* anything.

## 2. The potter has no noun

Hebrew never coined a word for "potter." The potter is *yotser*, the qal participle of יָצַר *yatsar* (H3335), "the forming one" — the same verb that forms the man from dust in Genesis 2:7 and the light in Isaiah 45:7. The verb occurs 44 times, and Isaiah owns it, 23 of the 44. Whether an English Bible prints "potter" or "Creator" at any given occurrence is a rendering choice, not a lexical one, and the corpus tagging betrays the same instability from the inside: at Jeremiah 18:4 and Psalm 2:9 the participle is split out as its own lemma, H3335a "the potter," while the identical participle at Isaiah 64:7 (MT) stays filed under the plain verb, glossed "the one who formed us." One word keeps crossing the potter/Creator line inside a single tagging scheme.

The naming runs in the opposite direction from ours. Greek κεραμεύς is built on κέραμος, potter's earth, fired ware; English "potter" is built on "pot." Those languages name the craftsman from his finished product — the hard thing at the end. Hebrew names him from the act: the wet stage, the hands still on. A language quietly decides which moment of the craft the craftsman *is*, and Hebrew chose the moment when the material can still move.

## 3. The word that became a pot

One step from the verb sits its noun, יֵצֶר *yetser* (H3336), nine occurrences, and it lives a double life: five times "inclination," twice "form," and once — Isaiah 29:16, the taunt verse itself — a physical pot. The same word is the evil inclination of every human thought in Genesis 6:5 and the frame God remembers is dust in Psalm 103:14: what a person is shaped *from* and what a person is shaped *toward*, sharing one noun. The Talmudic reading of Genesis 2:7's doubled yod as the two *yetsarim*, the good and evil inclinations, is midrash, not philology — but the consonantal fact underneath it is corpus-verified: the forming of the man carries וַיִּיצֶר with two yods, and the control verse, the forming of the beasts at 2:19, carries only one.

The synagogue eventually welded the two nouns into a single sentence. The piyyut *Ki Hinei Kachomer*, sung as Yom Kippur begins, sets Jeremiah's image — "as clay in the hand of the potter, who widens or contracts it at will, so are we in Your hand" — against the refrain "look to the covenant, and do not regard the *yetser*." Clay and inclination, H2563b and H3336, in one breath: remake the material, the liturgy asks, and overlook what it keeps inclining toward. That is outside this corpus, but it is the corpus's own two nouns doing the work.

## 4. Who owns which word

The Hebrew for the potter's clay hides behind a homograph. Bare חֹמֶר (H2563) is a dry measure, the homer; clay-*chomer* is filed at H2563b, seventeen occurrences — and their distribution is a finding about ownership: Job seven, Isaiah five, Jeremiah two. Isaiah owns the potter; *Job owns the clay*. Only in Job does the clay speak in the first person — 10:9 above, and Elihu leveling himself at 33:6, "I was also formed from clay." The same word is Babel's mortar (Genesis 11:3) and Egypt's brick-slavery (Exodus 1:14): building material for empires, body material for one scraped man on an ash heap.

The other clay words sort by register. חֶרֶשׂ (H2789) is the fired thing, earthenware and potsherd, and Leviticus owns it — always as the vessel that must be *broken* when defiled (Leviticus 11:33, 15:12), never washed. טִיט (H2916) is trodden mire: Isaiah 41:25 puts the potter in the conquest register, the one from the north trampling rulers "like a potter who treads the clay" — and Jeremiah, the corpus's one potter-watching prophet, ends up sunk in that same word at the bottom of a cistern (Jeremiah 38:6). And in Aramaic the whole vocabulary belongs to Daniel 2: חֲסַף, nine occurrences, every one of them in the statue vision, with a single Aramaic potter (Daniel 2:41). Counted by English gloss Daniel is the top "clay" book in the Bible, seventeen tokens — though the survey flags six of those as a tokenization artifact, a stray Aramaic determiner swept in as a clay-gloss carrier. Even corrected, the point stands: in Daniel clay is not creatureliness but the brittleness of empire, iron and clay that "will not adhere to one another."

## 5. Two chapters and a kiln

Jeremiah 18 and 19 are a staged pair, and everything turns on what state the clay is in. In chapter 18 the prophet watches wet clay at the wheel: "the vessel... became flawed in his hand; so he formed it into another vessel." The corpus lemma under "flawed" is the heavy one — נִשְׁחַת, niphal of שָׁחַת (H7843), the corrupt/destroy verb, and the link the survey flagged is now tool-verified: Genesis 6:12 carries the same lemma twice of the flood-ruined earth ("it was corrupt... all flesh had corrupted its way"). The grammar is making Jeremiah's point for him: the vessel on the wheel is described with the vocabulary of the flood — and reworked anyway. At the wheel, even *destroyed* is a provisional state. In chapter 19 the same prophet buys a *fired* jar and smashes it at Topheth: "like one shatters a potter's jar that can never again be repaired."

The line between the two chapters is a real threshold in the material. Wet clay owes its reworkability to films of water between mineral plates; even bone-dry unfired clay can be slaked back to plasticity with water. But fire the piece past roughly 500 °C and the clay minerals surrender their chemically bound water — dehydroxylation — and no amount of soaking ever restores the plastic state. Reversibility does not fade gradually; it ends at the kiln, which is exactly where Jeremiah staged its ending. Sirach knew the kiln as the moment of judgment from the craft side: the furnace is where the potter's vessels are proved (Sirach 27:5).

Two footnotes from the potter's yard sharpen the oracle rather than softening it. First: fired clay is not actually useless — potters have always ground broken sherds into grog and kneaded it into fresh clay as temper. What the kiln makes irreversible is not the *stuff* but the *vessel*; the material survives, the form does not. Jeremiah 19 aims precisely at the form: a jar, never again repaired. Lamentations 4:2 runs the same devaluation in one line — Zion's sons, once fine gold, now reckoned as potter's jars. Second: the sherd had an afterlife the oracles don't mention. Broken pottery was the ancient scrap paper, and the ostraca from Lachish — Hebrew military dispatches inked on potsherds during the very Babylonian campaign Jeremiah preached through — are among the last written words of the kingdom the smashed jar stood for. Isaiah 30:14 promises a shattering so total no shard remains big enough to scoop coals; it is shards that carried the archive out.

## 6. The wheel goes quiet

Cross into Greek and the potter has almost vanished. κεραμεύς occurs three times: once in Romans 9:21, twice in Matthew 27 — where the potter is no maker at all, merely the former owner of a field bought with betrayal money, closing the loop from Zechariah 11:13's thirty pieces thrown "to the potter" in the LORD's house. (Training knowledge says the LXX reads "foundry" there and the Syriac "treasury" against the MT's "potter" — but this corpus carries no LXX layer to check it, so that stays a flag, not a fact.) Both named potter-places in the corpus end as graveyards: Topheth, and Akeldama, the field of blood. In the New Testament God never once sits at the wheel; the potter survives only inside quotation (Revelation 2:27's κεραμικός, riding the Psalm 2:9 line about shattering the nations like a *yotser's* vessel) and inside argument (Romans 9).

Meanwhile the one genuinely new clay-act in the whole corpus runs the image backward. πηλός, "clay," occurs six times in the New Testament, and five of them are John 9: Jesus spitting, kneading mud, spreading it on a blind man's eyes. Spittle-cures were a recognized healing gesture in that world — Tacitus has Vespasian curing a blind man with spittle at Alexandria — but the *clay* is John's addition, and it is remedial: clay applied *to* a man, not a man made *from* clay. Reading the scene as a deliberate echo of Genesis 2:7, the Creator resuming hand-work on unfinished eyes, is patristic tradition, not corpus data; the corpus supplies only the shared word, and the reversal of direction.

Paul, for his part, lets the lump escape the argument it was made for. The "lump" of Romans 9:21 is φύραμα, and its only other occurrence in Romans is 11:16 — "if the firstfruit is holy, so is the lump." Within one letter the same word moves from the wheel, where vessels of wrath and mercy are decreed, to the kitchen, where holiness spreads through dough. And behind Romans 9 stands the Wisdom of Solomon, which states the argument a step earlier — one clay, vessels for clean uses and their contraries, "the potter is the judge" (Wisdom 15:7) — then swings it against the idol-maker: the potter, himself "but a little before... made of earth," molds a god from the same clay and must soon give back the soul which was lent him. That Paul knew Wisdom is standard scholarship, not corpus data; but the verbal skeleton — same clay, two contrary uses, potter as judge — is visible in the corpus text itself. Sirach holds down the determinist maximum ("as the clay of the potter in his hand... so men are in the hand of him who made them," 33:13, placed straight after Adam-from-dust) — and also the craft's dignity: the potter at his kick-wheel, excluded from the council, yet of him and his fellow artisans, "they maintain the fabric of the age; their prayer is in the handiwork of their craft" (38:29-34).

## 7. Keeping the clay, losing the potter

The prose classics complete the attrition: they keep the clay and drop the potter. Augustine rejects the artificer analogy outright — God is not like a human maker who "invests with a form what already existeth... as clay, or stone" (Confessions 11.7); creation from nothing has no wheel and no prior lump. Yet he cannot do without the material: the incarnation is "a lowly habitation of our clay" (7.24), and Monica delays his baptism preferring "to expose to them the clay whence I might afterwards be moulded, than the very cast, when made" (1.18) — wet-state theology in a mother's decision. And the wheel itself returns in Augustine for one last job, arguing about *time*: if the heavenly bodies stood still and a potter's wheel kept turning, would its turning not still measure time (11.29)? Time, he concludes, is not the motion of the heavens. Sixteen centuries later metrology conceded the point: since 1967 the second has been defined not by any celestial motion but by 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a cesium-133 atom — timekeeping formally detached from the heavens and handed to the humbler motions of matter. The potter's wheel was always a good enough clock.

Julian of Norwich uses clay to draw the body/soul frontier — the body from "the clay of earth," but for the soul God "would take right nought" (62.7). Chesterton's sculptor "is glad that the clay is colourless": creation loving its limits (Orthodoxy 3.23). Clement of Rome quotes Job's "houses of clay" at length (1 Clement 39.1). And Brother Lawrence and Barnabas contain no potter and no clay at all — a genuine thin layer, logged as such; the theme's prose weight sits almost entirely in Augustine and à Kempis.

## Coda: what state, and whose move

The survey leaves one wire exposed, and this piece will not insulate it. Is the vessel's use assigned at the wheel — the potter making from one lump a vessel for honor and one for dishonor (Romans 9:21), men in the hand of their maker as clay in the potter's (Sirach 33:13), the potter as judge (Wisdom 15:7)? Or can the vessel change its own use — "if anyone purges himself, he will be a vessel for honor" (2 Timothy 2:21), in the very σκεῦος-εἰς-τιμήν vocabulary of Romans 9? Jeremiah 18 sits on both sides at once: the reforming of the marred pot is entirely the potter's act at the wheel — and yet the oracle's application turns and commands the nation to repent (18:8, 18:11), as though the clay's turning and the potter's re-turning were somehow one motion.

The corpus rebukes the clay that questions and answers the clay that prays. It never says which of those the purged vessel is doing. What is the clay allowed to say — and has anything it said ever turned the wheel?

### Outside-corpus assertions

Training-knowledge welds not checkable against this corpus; these rest on the model's confidence and should be editor-reviewed, not tool-verified:

- Etymology: Greek κεραμεύς is derived from κέραμος (potter's earth / fired ware); English "potter" from "pot." The contrast with the Hebrew participle's act-naming is the weld.
- Ceramics chemistry: unfired clay (even bone-dry greenware) can be slaked back to plastic clay with water; firing past roughly 450-600 °C drives off chemically bound hydroxyl water (dehydroxylation of clay minerals such as kaolinite), after which plasticity is unrecoverable.
- Grog: crushed fired sherds are kneaded into fresh clay as temper, a practice attested in ancient wares (grog-tempered pottery).
- Lachish ostraca: Hebrew military dispatches written on potsherds during the final Babylonian campaign against Judah (c. 589-586 BC).
- *Ki Hinei Kachomer*: piyyut sung at the start of Yom Kippur; stanza "like clay in the hand of the potter" with the refrain "look to the covenant, and do not regard the *yetser*."
- Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81: Vespasian at Alexandria heals a blind man using spittle.
- SI second: redefined in 1967 as 9,192,631,770 periods of the cesium-133 hyperfine transition, replacing astronomical definitions.
- Inherited flag (survey claim 10): LXX Zech 11:13 "foundry/furnace" and Syriac "treasury" against MT "potter" — asserted here only as an unverifiable training-knowledge note; both corpus translations read "potter" and no LXX layer exists to check.
